## Context

Each joist has steel inside, and the diameter of that steel depends on its
length: the longer the joist, the thicker the steel. The plant had **three
production beds**; each bed always produced **three 33-meter rows at once**,
nine rows per day, which were then cut to the lengths demanded: about five
different lengths, the longest around 10 meters.

## The problem

When long and short joists were produced on the same bed, the short ones got the
steel of the long ones: "super joists", much stronger than needed. That excess
of steel, the most expensive input, added to the leftover offcuts, which were
also waste.

## What I did

I owned this work from start to finish. I identified it as a **cutting stock
problem**, which I had studied in Operations Research, and formulated it as an
**integer linear programming** model: minimize the steel used, subject to
meeting the demand for each length.

1. **I generated the cutting patterns.** For each steel diameter, the
   combinations of pieces that fit in a 33 m row, given that a joist can carry
   steel equal to or thicker than it needs, never thinner. Only maximal
   patterns count: those that can't fit another piece.
2. **I built the model.** The variables are the number of rows cut with each
   pattern and the number of beds produced with each diameter. The goal is to
   minimize the cost of steel; the constraints are meeting the demand for each
   length and respecting that a bed always produces its three rows with the
   same diameter.
3. **I implemented the algorithm in PHP** that solved the model and produced the
   optimal plan: how many beds to produce with each diameter and how to cut each
   row, with the least leftover and the least steel waste.

A detail the model finds on its own: a single objective function penalizes both
oversized steel and offcuts, since you pay for the steel of the whole row. But
it can tell when it pays to **use a leftover** with an oversized joist: in the
demo scenario, cutting 3 × 10 m + 1 × 3 m costs the same as 3 × 10 m and
delivers one more joist.

## Results

- Planning stopped relying on intuition.
- Each joist is produced with the steel its length requires.
- Consumption of the most expensive input on the line went down.

## An example with numbers

For the demo I built an example with five lengths based on common commercial
sizes, extended up to 10 m, and a demand of 465 joists. The numbers are **illustrative, not measured at the
plant**: the optimal plan uses 24 beds (8 production days), sits just 4% above
the theoretical minimum of steel, and uses about 37% less steel than filling the
beds in the order joists arrive.
